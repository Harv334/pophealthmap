Methodology and technical notes

← Back to the map · Accessibility · PopHealth Map, London

This page records where each figure comes from, which geography and year it belongs to, and what it cannot tell you. It is written to be read alongside the map rather than instead of it.

This tool is in development. The data is real and comes from published sources, but the map is still being built and checked, so treat figures as provisional.

The words used on this map

Official statistics use short names for areas and measures. Where the map uses one, this says what it means. Where a plain word is clearer, the map uses that instead and this gives the official name for it.

Geographies, and which year each one is from

The four geographies on the map come from different censuses and boundary reviews. They do not nest inside one another, and that has consequences worth understanding before comparing them.

MSOAs can now be drawn as well as aggregated through. They are a layer of their own in the sidebar rather than a third position on the ward and LSOA switch, because they nest into neither: an MSOA is built from whole LSOAs, but 41% of LSOAs cross a ward line, so an MSOA edge agreeing with a ward edge is a coincidence rather than a rule. The layer is fetched only when you switch it on. An MSOA is in scope when at least one of its LSOAs is, which is the same definition the rest of the pipeline works to.

All boundary layers are the BGC generalisation: simplified to 20 metres and clipped to the coastline. Keeping them at the same generalisation matters. LSOAs were previously taken at BSC, 200 metres, which is ten times coarser, and because the two had been simplified independently their shared borders no longer coincided: LSOA edges visibly cut across ward lines.

Coordinates in the files the browser downloads are rounded to five decimal places, which moves no point by more than 0.32 metres and roughly halves the file size.

What can go wrong when you read a figure

Six things on this map are easy to read the wrong way. Each one is a property of how the figures are published, not a fault, and each changes what a number means.

1. LSOAs do not sit inside wards

LSOA boundaries are from 2021 and wards were redrawn in 2025, so the two genuinely cross. 59% of LSOAs sit entirely within one ward. 41% cross a ward line. Every LSOA is assigned to exactly one ward, using the ONS best-fit lookup LSOA21_WD25_LAD25_EW_LU_v2, so a ward figure counts each LSOA once in the ward it best fits, and not in proportion to the area it shares. LSOA outlines crossing ward outlines at high zoom is the geography being honest, not a drawing error.

2. Only counts are added up

Everything else is averaged and weighted by population. LSOA figures reach wards that way, and wards reach boroughs the same way, except for genuinely additive measures such as population, crime totals and service counts, which are summed. MSOA figures reach wards through the LSOAs the two share, so a ward straddling two MSOAs gets a blend in proportion to where its people are.

3. Some health figures are a borough figure repeated

The health data comes from OHID Fingertips in two pulls. The Local Health set is published per MSOA and varies ward by ward. The borough set is published per local authority and is written unchanged onto every ward in that borough, so 18 of the health indicators are identical for every ward in a borough. Ranking wards on one of those produces a real-looking order that is an artefact of the geography rather than a finding. The map flags them, and the indicator list shows how many distinct values an indicator actually has.

4. Missing is not zero

A blank ward can mean the figure was withheld, not that it is zero. Fingertips suppresses MSOA figures where small counts could identify individuals. In Query, a ward with no figure for one of your conditions can neither pass nor fail it, so it is left out of the results and the number left out is reported underneath them.

5. Colour follows the indicator, not the size of the number

The highest IMD decile is the least deprived tenth, and the highest life expectancy is the best outcome, so colouring the largest value red would state the opposite of the truth. For the Local Health set the direction is OHID’s own published polarity, carried through from the source. Where an indicator has no better or worse end, it is left uncoloured. The share of residents over 65, or in social renting, or of any ethnic group, is a fact about who lives there rather than a good or bad result.

6. Service counts are attributed by where the service stands

Pharmacies use the pipeline’s own ward count. Schools, libraries and community centres use the ward code their source file already carries. Everything else is placed by testing its point against the ward polygon. A service used by a neighbouring ward is still counted in the ward it stands in, so these will not always agree with a count made a different way.

Known gaps

  • City of London. Its 20 wards have no deprivation score and no Local Health indicators. They are tiny and share very few LSOAs, so there is nothing to aggregate. This is a property of the geography, not a fetch failure.
  • Five layers cover North West London only: schools, community centres, libraries, ESOL providers and CICs. They are badged NWL on the map. Blank elsewhere means not yet collected, not none.
  • 39 renumbered MSOAs. Fingertips still publishes the Local Health set against 2011 MSOA codes. Where a code no longer matches a 2021 one, the value is left missing rather than filled from a neighbour.
  • DWP benefits are not included. The figures are on DWP Stat-Xplore, which needs a registered API key. NOMIS holds nothing newer than November 2018 for them.

Rebuilding this

The whole map is rebuilt by one script, fetch_all_data.py, with no manual downloads. Sources, licences and per-source notes are in the repository: github.com/Harv334/pophealthmap.
