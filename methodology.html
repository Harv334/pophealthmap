Methodology and technical notes

PopHealth Map puts more than 100 published indicators on one map of England, from the Census, the NHS, the Index of Multiple Deprivation and other official sources. This page explains where each one comes from, how it was placed on wards and neighbourhoods, and what it can and cannot tell you, so you can judge how far to rely on it. If you only have a few minutes, start with the five things to know at the top of How to interpret the data, and you will find the exact ONS lookup files in the technical appendix at the end.

PopHealth Map is in beta. Every figure comes from an official published source, and this page explains how each one is placed onto wards and neighbourhoods. If a figure looks wrong, please use Report a problem on its card or write to contact@pophealth.uk, and we will check it against the publisher's own release.

How to cite this map

When you use a figure from this map, please name the organisation that published it as well as PopHealth Map, because the publisher's own release is the authority for that figure. Each indicator's card gives its source, the period it covers and the level it is published at, and we suggest a citation in this form:

PopHealth Map, indicator for area, period. Figures from publisher, updated on the date shown at the foot of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on date.

For example, a citation for one of the Local Health figures would read:

PopHealth Map, Life expectancy at birth, women, for the wards of Tower Hamlets, 2019 to 2023, updated on the date shown at the foot of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on the day you looked. Ward estimates calculated by PopHealth Map from OHID Local Health MSOA figures.

To show someone exactly what you saw, use Copy link at the top of the map, which keeps the indicator, the area and the level you had chosen. Most of the figures are published under the Open Government Licence, which lets you reuse them as long as you credit the source, and Licences and credits gives the wording each publisher asks for.

How to interpret the data

Five things to know before you use a figure

  • Darker usually means a higher figure, whether that is good or bad, except for the deprivation deciles and ranks, where darker always means more deprived. The ends of the legend are the 5th and 95th percentiles rather than the highest and lowest areas.
  • Each indicator has its own period, which can be several years old. The date at the foot of the map says when we last updated the figures, not the years they describe.
  • A blank area has no figure, which is not the same as a figure of zero.
  • Each figure is published for one level of area, and figures for the other levels are calculated from it, so a ward figure is usually a close estimate rather than an exact count. The card for each indicator says which level its publisher uses.
  • Two indicators that rise and fall together are a place to look, not proof that one causes the other.

Each of these comes from how the figures are published rather than from an error, and the sections below explain them, grouped by the question they answer. The names of the areas, from the local authority (LA) down to the LSOA and the output area, are explained in the Glossary of geographies and measures.

What a figure measures

Every indicator has a card

The About this indicator button under the chosen indicator, or the key along the bottom of the map, opens a card that explains what is counted and out of what, the level its publisher releases it at and how it reaches the other levels, the period, the source and how often the source updates it. For the Local Health figures, the card uses OHID's own description of each figure, including its unit, whether its years are calendar or financial years, and when OHID last updated it.

Each indicator has its own period

The period beside a legend is the one the publisher gives for that indicator, and it differs a great deal from one indicator to the next. The date at the foot of the map says when we last updated the figures rather than the period they describe, and because each update takes the newest figures published, some indicators describe years well before it.

Older figures and the lockdown Census

Indicators whose period ended more than three years ago are marked in the picker and the legend with the year they end, so that a figure for 2016/17 to 2020/21 is not read as current. Census 2021 indicators are not marked, because it is the latest Census, but its travel to work figures carry their own warning: the Census was taken on 21 March 2021, during a lockdown, and ONS advises that they do not show normal travel patterns.

Several Local Health periods take in the pandemic. Deaths and life expectancy pooled over 2019 to 2023 include 2020 and 2021, when COVID-19 raised deaths most in more deprived areas, so gaps between places may be wider than in a normal year. The hospital admissions series ends in 2020/21, the first year of the pandemic, when emergency admissions fell sharply. The cards for these indicators say so.

Life expectancy is shown for women and men separately

Local Health publishes life expectancy at birth for women and for men, with no figure for everyone together, so the map offers it as two indicators.

What the colours show

Darker means a higher figure

Every indicator is shaded from light for its lowest figures to dark for its highest, whether a higher figure is good news or bad. The exception is the deprivation deciles and ranks, which count down from 1 for the most deprived, so on those maps the colours are turned round and darker still means more deprived. The Highest and lowest 10 lists and the printable reports do say whether a higher figure is better or worse, and for the Local Health figures they follow the direction OHID publishes. Indicators that describe who lives in an area, such as the share of residents over 65, in social housing or of any ethnic group, have no better or worse end, so they are listed as highest and lowest rather than best and worst.

The ends of the legend are not the highest and lowest areas

The colours run from the 5th to the 95th percentile of the areas drawn, so that one extreme area does not use up the whole scale, and every area beyond either end takes the end colour. Where any area does, the legend marks that end with ≤ or ≥, so "≥ 37.8" means 37.8 or more, and the Highest and lowest 10 lists give the real figures.

Where a figure sits

Hovering over an area, or tapping one on a phone, tells you where its figure sits among the other areas of the same level in the region you are looking at, for example "higher than 82% of London's LSOAs". It is a position within that region rather than within England, so it changes if you change region.

Two indicators at once

Compare with a second indicator shades the map by two indicators together. Each one is split into thirds, or as near to thirds as tied figures allow, across the areas of the level being drawn (so the thirds of the North East's LSOAs rather than England's), and each area takes one of nine colours for its pair. An indicator that too many areas share one figure of, so that a third would be empty, is not offered as the second, and an area missing either figure is drawn white with a dashed edge. Because the thirds depend on the areas on screen, the same LSOA can fall in a different third when you look at a different region. A pattern on this map is a place to look, not evidence that one thing causes the other, because two indicators can move together when both follow deprivation, age or how urban an area is.

How sure a figure is

Missing is not zero

An area with no figure is missing it rather than recording zero, either because OHID withholds MSOA figures where small counts could identify someone, or because an older measure has no figure where boundaries changed in 2021 (see MSOAs redrawn in 2021). In Query, an area with no figure for one of your conditions can neither pass nor fail it, so it is left out of the results and the number left out is shown underneath them.

Better, worse or similar to England

OHID tests every Local Health MSOA figure against England, and if the area's 95% confidence interval does not include England's value, the difference is unlikely to be down to chance. Hovering over an MSOA shows OHID's verdict beside the figure. Many MSOA figures rest on small numbers, so an area that looks high on the colour scale is often similar to England by this test. Ward and LA figures are calculated from MSOA figures and carry no test, and neither do the few MSOA figures estimated from 2011 areas.

How a figure reaches an area

The full method is under How figures are placed onto areas, and these are the parts of it that change what you see.

LSOAs do not sit inside wards

LSOA boundaries come from the 2021 Census and wards were redrawn in 2025, so the two genuinely cross, and about four in ten LSOAs cross a ward line. Each LSOA is given to the one ward it best fits, so a ward's figure counts each LSOA once rather than in proportion to the land it shares. So if you zoom in and see LSOA and ward edges crossing, that is how the two sets of boundaries are really drawn, not a fault in the map.

How figures are combined into wards and LAs

When small areas are aggregated into wards or LAs, rates, percentages and scores are weighted by population, so each area counts in proportion to the number of people who live there. Only raw totals, such as population, crimes and the number of services, are summed directly. Census percentages are combined from their output areas in this way too, as described under Census figures at every level.Census percentages are the exception, because ONS publishes the counts behind them: each is calculated from the summed counts of the output areas, as described under Census figures at every level. LSOA figures reach wards in this way, and MSOA figures reach wards through the LSOAs the two share, so a ward that straddles two MSOAs gets a blend in proportion to where its people live. Where a figure is not published for LAs, each LA's figure is calculated from its wards in the same way, which makes it the rate for the LA's residents rather than an average of unequal wards. It can differ slightly from a figure the council or a publisher gives for itself, which may use a different population or year. For OHID's Local Health indicators the difference can be larger, because life expectancy and standardised ratios cannot be averaged exactly and OHID's own LA figures can cover a different period, so the map says under its key that these LA figures are our estimates, leaves the City of London out of their LA rankings, and the Area profile shows OHID's own LA figures. Where the data carries OHID's LA figure for one of these indicators, the map uses it instead.

Figures published only for a whole LA are not offered on the map

Some health figures, such as smoking, obesity and healthy life expectancy from OHID's main profiles, are published once for each whole LA. Every ward in an LA would carry the same figure, and ranking wards on it would produce an order that looks real but only reflects the geography, so these indicators are not offered in the indicator list. Ward profiles and reports still show them, labelled as the LA's figure.

Services are counted where they stand

GP practices, pharmacies, dentists and hospitals are counted in the ward whose boundary they sit inside. A practice that serves a neighbouring ward still counts in the ward it stands in, so these counts will not always agree with one made a different way.

Primary care networks are lists of patients, not areas

A primary care network (PCN) is a group of GP practices, and its population is the people registered with them, wherever they live. Which practices belong to which network comes from OHID, which follows NHS England's list, and where their patients live comes from NHS Digital's Patients Registered at a GP Practice by LSOA. When those registration figures are available, choosing a network shades every LSOA with five or more of its patients by the share of that LSOA's registered residents who use it, so overlapping catchments show as they are. The PCN footprints outline gives each LSOA to the network most of its registered residents use, which makes a convenient boundary but not one that NHS England publishes or that practices work to.

What the map covers

The map covers all of England. Each of the nine English regions is built separately, from the same sources and in the same way, and you can switch between them on the map: London, the North East, the North West, Yorkshire and The Humber, the East Midlands, the West Midlands, the East of England, the South East and the South West.

The England view

The England view puts all nine regions together on one map at every level, with two differences from a single region. Boundaries are simplified a little further so that about 7,000 wards can still be shown in a browser, which moves a ward, LSOA or MSOA edge by no more than about 25 metres and an LA outline by no more than about 100 metres. An indicator that only one region has, such as London's public transport accessibility level, is left out rather than shown as a mostly empty map. The charities directory stays with each region, because for the whole country it is too large to open at once.

When the figures are updated

We update London's figures on the 15th of each month, and every other region the same morning, after which the England view is put together again from the updated regions. How figures are placed onto areas

Figures arrive from many publishers, for different years and different kinds of area, and boundaries change: wards are redrawn, councils merge, and the small Census areas were revised in 2021. So that every figure is put on the map in the same way, and every step can be checked against a published ONS file, the map follows one rule.

One rule for every figure

Every figure is first placed on the 2021 Census building blocks, which are output areas, LSOAs and MSOAs, and is then combined into wards and LAs using the official ONS lookup files listed in the technical appendix, with counts summed, Census percentages calculated from their summed counts, and everything else weighted by population. Nothing is matched by name, and no boundary is laid over another to decide where a figure belongs.

Best fit, not exact fit

The ONS lookups give each LSOA to the single ward it mostly sits in, judged by where people live. Because many LSOAs cross a ward line (see LSOAs do not sit inside wards), a ward figure built in this way is a close estimate for the ward rather than an exact count, but because the same lookup is used for every indicator, ward figures can still be compared fairly with one another. For a few wards, though, the LSOAs given to them hold noticeably more or fewer people than the ward itself, and those wards, where the LSOAs hold under 80% or over 125% of the ward's residents, are marked in the Area profile and the ward report.

Census figures at every level

ONS publishes Census 2021 figures for output areas, LSOAs and MSOAs, and the map shows ONS's own figure at each of those levels, so they need no matching at all. Census figures for wards are calculated from output areas rather than from LSOAs, using ONS's output area to ward best fit lookup, because output areas are about a fifth the size of LSOAs and follow ward boundaries much more closely. Each ward percentage is an average of the percentages of the output areas in the ward, weighted by their residents, so a measure of households, such as the share with no car or van, is weighted by residents too.Each ward percentage is calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in the ward: the households with no car or van in those output areas, for example, divided by all their households. A measure of households is therefore a share of the ward's households, and a measure of people a share of its residents. LA figures are an average of the ward figures, weighted by population.LA figures are calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in each LA's wards. To see output areas, choose a Census indicator and OA on the level switch, and their borders appear as you zoom in. An output area holds about 125 households, so a single household can move a percentage noticeably, and these figures are best read as a picture of a street or estate rather than a precise measure.

MSOAs

MSOAs have their own place on the level switch. An MSOA is made of whole LSOAs, but because many LSOAs cross a ward line (see LSOAs do not sit inside wards), MSOAs and wards do not fit inside one another, and where an MSOA edge matches a ward edge it does so by coincidence. Local Health figures are published for MSOAs, and they reach wards through the LSOAs the two share.

Figures estimated from 2011 areas are marked

Some Local Health figures are still published for the MSOAs of 2011. Where a 2011 area was split or merged to make a 2021 one, the figure for the 2021 area is calculated from the 2011 ones, weighted by how many people lived in each, and the map says Estimated from 2011 areas beside it and does not compare it with England, because the publisher's comparison applied to the old area. At present OHID publishes no figure at all for the areas that changed, so they are left blank and nothing on the map is estimated in this way.

LA codes are checked at every update

Each update checks that every LA on the map was found under its current code in the publisher's figures, because an LA that has merged or changed its code since the figures were published would otherwise go missing without anyone noticing. Any LA that does not match is flagged so that we can put it right.

Known gaps

City of London

The City of London has 25 wards, but its few LSOAs are each matched to one of just five of them, so the other 20 wards have no deprivation score, no Local Health figures and none of the other figures built from LSOAs. Census figures are calculated from output areas instead, so some of those 20 wards do have them. This comes from the geography rather than from a missed update, and you can choose LA to see the figures for the City as a whole.

MSOAs redrawn in 2021

The older Local Health figures, such as the hospital admissions for 2016/17 to 2020/21, are published for the MSOAs of 2011, and OHID leaves out the MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021, which is about 2% of England's and 39 of London's. Those areas are blank for those figures rather than estimated, and the map's notice and the area's hover card say so, rather than giving privacy as the reason.

The newer figures, such as deaths and life expectancy for 2019 to 2023, are published for the MSOAs of 2021, so boundary changes leave no gaps in them. OHID still withholds some MSOA figures where numbers are small, for example men's life expectancy for 52 of London's MSOAs, and where that leaves a ward with figures for fewer than four in five of its residents, we show no figure for the ward (see Missing is not zero). A ward figure that rests on most but not all of its residents says what share it covers.

Public transport accessibility is London only

The public transport accessibility level (PTAL) is TfL's measure and has no national equivalent, so every other region has the four station and bus stop measures instead.

The GLA publishes PTAL for the LSOAs of 2011. Each LSOA drawn in 2021 takes the figure of the 2011 area it was split from, through the lookup ONS publishes, and a merged LSOA takes the average of the areas it was made from. The figures on the map now were made before that lookup was in place, so the 335 LSOAs drawn in 2021, around Stratford, the Royal Docks, Wembley Park and Colindale among other places, have no figure until the next update. A ward with figures for fewer than four in five of its residents is left blank.

Charities are listed region by region

The charities directory is not part of the England view, so open a region to see it.

PCN catchments are not always available

The figures for where each network's patients live come from an NHS Digital file that we add by hand at each update, because it cannot be downloaded automatically. Until the latest file has been added, a network shows its practices but no catchment.

Benefits other than the claimant count

The map does not yet include benefits other than the claimant count, such as Universal Credit, Personal Independence Payment, Employment and Support Allowance, Carer's Allowance and Pension Credit. Current figures for them by LSOA are published only through DWP's Stat-Xplore service, because Nomis has either never carried them or stops in 2018 on older boundaries. The claimant count, which covers people claiming benefits because they are unemployed, is included.
