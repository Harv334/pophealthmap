Methodology and technical notes

PopHealth Map shows more than 100 published figures on one map of England. They come from the Census, the NHS, the Index of Multiple Deprivation and other official sources. This page explains where each figure comes from, how we fit it to wards and neighbourhoods, and what it can and cannot tell you. If you are short of time, read the five things to know at the start of How to interpret the data. The exact ONS files we use are in the technical appendix at the end.

PopHealth Map is in beta, which means it is new and still being tested. If a figure looks wrong, please use Report a problem on its card or email contact@pophealth.uk, and we will check it against the publisher's own figures.

How to cite this map

When you use a figure from this map, please name the organisation that published it as well as PopHealth Map. The publisher's own release is the official source for that figure. Each indicator's card gives its source, the period it covers and the level of area it is published for. We suggest you cite it like this:

PopHealth Map, indicator for area, period. Figures from publisher, updated on the date shown at the foot of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on date.

For example, a citation for one of the Local Health figures would read:

PopHealth Map, Life expectancy at birth, women, for the wards of Tower Hamlets, 2019 to 2023, updated on the date shown at the foot of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on the day you looked. Ward estimates calculated by PopHealth Map from OHID Local Health MSOA figures.

To show someone exactly what you saw, use Copy link at the top of the map. The link keeps the indicator, the area and the level you chose. Most of the figures are published under the Open Government Licence. This lets you reuse them as long as you say where they came from, and Licences and credits gives the wording each publisher asks for.

How to interpret the data

Five things to know before you use a figure

  • Darker usually means a higher figure, whether that is good or bad. The exception is the deprivation deciles and ranks, where darker always means more deprived. The two ends of the legend (the colour key) are not the highest and lowest areas. They are set so that 5% of areas fall below one end and 5% are above the other.
  • Each indicator covers its own period, and some are several years old. The date at the foot of the map says when we last updated the figures, not the years they describe.
  • A blank area has no figure. That is not the same as a figure of zero.
  • Each figure is published for one level of area, and we work out the other levels from it. So a ward figure is usually a close estimate, not an exact count. Each indicator's card says which level its publisher uses.
  • When two indicators rise and fall together, that is a reason to look closer. It does not prove that one causes the other.

None of these are mistakes. They come from the way the figures are published, and the sections below explain each one. The types of area, from the local authority (LA) down to the LSOA and the output area, are explained in the Glossary of geographies and measures.

What a figure measures

Every indicator has a card

The About this indicator button under the chosen indicator, or the key along the bottom of the map, opens a card for that indicator. The card says what is counted and out of what total. It gives the level of area the publisher uses and how we reach the other levels. It also gives the period, the source and how often the source is updated. For the Local Health figures, which come from OHID (the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities), the card uses OHID's own description. This includes the unit, whether the years are calendar or financial years, and when OHID last updated the figure.

Each indicator has its own period

The period next to a legend is the one the publisher gives for that indicator, and it can be very different from one indicator to the next. The date at the foot of the map is when we last updated the figures, not the period they describe. Each update takes the newest figures published, but some of those still describe years well before the update.

Older figures and the lockdown Census

If an indicator's period ended more than three years ago, the indicator list and the legend show the year it ends. That way a figure for 2016/17 to 2020/21 is not mistaken for a current one. Census 2021 indicators are not marked, because it is the latest Census. Its travel to work figures do carry a warning, though. The Census was taken on 21 March 2021, during a lockdown, and ONS says those figures do not show normal travel patterns.

Several Local Health periods include the pandemic. Deaths and life expectancy for 2019 to 2023 include 2020 and 2021, when COVID-19 raised deaths most in more deprived areas. So the gaps between places may be wider than in a normal year. The hospital admissions figures end in 2020/21, the first year of the pandemic, when emergency admissions fell sharply. The cards for these indicators say so.

Life expectancy is shown for women and men separately

Local Health gives life expectancy at birth for women and for men, but not for everyone together. So the map shows it as two indicators.

What the colours show

Darker means a higher figure

Every indicator is shaded from light for its lowest figures to dark for its highest. This is true whether a high figure is good news or bad. The exception is the deprivation deciles and ranks. These count down from 1 for the most deprived, so on those maps we turn the colours round and darker still means more deprived. The Highest and lowest 10 lists and the printable reports do say whether a higher figure is better or worse. For the Local Health figures, they follow the direction OHID gives. Some indicators simply describe who lives in an area, such as the share of residents over 65, in social housing or of any ethnic group. These have no better or worse end, so they are listed as highest and lowest rather than best and worst.

The ends of the legend are not the highest and lowest areas

The colours run from the 5th percentile to the 95th percentile of the areas shown. The 5th percentile is the figure that 5% of areas fall below, and the 95th is the figure that 5% of areas are above. This stops one extreme area from using up the whole colour scale. Any area beyond either end takes the end colour. When that happens, the legend marks that end with ≤ or ≥, so "≥ 37.8" means 37.8 or more. The Highest and lowest 10 lists give the real figures.

Where a figure sits

If you hover over an area, or tap it on a phone, you can see where its figure sits among the other areas of the same level in the region you are looking at. For example, it might say "higher than 82% of London's LSOAs". This compares the area with its region, not with all of England, so it changes if you switch region.

How sure a figure is

Missing is not zero

When an area has no figure, the figure is missing. It does not mean zero. There are two reasons this happens. OHID holds back some MSOA figures where the numbers are so small that someone could be identified. And some older measures have no figure where boundaries changed in 2021 (see MSOAs redrawn in 2021). In Query, an area with no figure for one of your conditions can neither pass nor fail it. So it is left out of the results, and the number left out is shown underneath them.

Better, worse or similar to England

OHID checks every Local Health MSOA figure against the figure for England. It uses a 95% confidence interval, which is the range the true figure is very likely to be in. If that range does not include England's figure, the difference is unlikely to be down to chance. Hover over an MSOA to see OHID's result next to the figure. Many MSOA figures are based on small numbers, so an area that looks high on the colour scale is often still similar to England by this test. Ward and LA figures are worked out from MSOA figures and have no test. Neither do the few MSOA figures estimated from 2011 areas.

Modelled figures come with a range

Some figures are not counted but modelled. ONS's household income figures and its healthy and disability-free life expectancy for MSOAs are worked out from surveys and records, because too few people in each MSOA are surveyed to measure them directly. ONS gives each MSOA figure a 95% confidence interval, which is the range the true figure is very likely to be in. Hover over an MSOA to see it. Many neighbouring MSOAs have ranges that overlap, so they are not clearly different. Ward and LA figures are worked out from MSOA figures and have no range.

How a figure reaches an area

The full method is under How figures are placed onto areas. These are the parts of it that change what you see.

LSOAs do not sit inside wards

LSOA boundaries come from the 2021 Census, and wards were redrawn in 2025. So the two really do cross, and about four in ten LSOAs cross a ward boundary. We never split an LSOA. ONS publishes a best-fit lookup that puts each whole LSOA in the ward where most of its residents live, and we use it as it is (it is named in the technical appendix). This means a ward's figure counts each LSOA once, rather than splitting it by the land it shares. If you zoom in and see LSOA and ward edges crossing, that is how the boundaries are really drawn. It is not a fault in the map.

How figures are combined into wards and LAs

When we combine small areas into wards or LAs, counts such as population, the claimant count and the number of services are added up. Rates, percentages and scores are weighted by population. This means each small area counts in proportion to the number of people living there at the 2021 Census. Fuel poverty is a share of households, so it is weighted by the number of households instead. Household income is an average over households, so it is weighted by households too. Counts of stations within 1 km and bus stops within 800 metres are averaged, not added, because one station is close to several LSOAs. So a ward's figure is what its average resident has nearby. Census percentages are combined from their output areas by weighting too, as explained under Census figures at every level.Census percentages are different, because ONS publishes the counts behind them. Each one is calculated from the summed counts of the output areas, as explained under Census figures at every level. This is how LSOA figures reach wards. MSOA figures reach wards through the LSOAs the two share, so a ward that sits across two MSOAs gets a mix of the two, in proportion to where its people live.

Where a figure is not published for LAs, we work out each LA's figure from its wards in the same way. This gives the rate for all of the LA's residents, rather than a simple average of wards of different sizes. Fuel poverty is worked out from all of the LA's LSOAs, weighted by households, and household income from all of the LA's MSOAs, weighted by households. An LA figure worked out like this can differ slightly from a figure the council or a publisher gives, which may use a different population or year. For OHID's Local Health indicators the difference can be bigger. Life expectancy and ratios compared with England cannot be averaged exactly, and OHID's own LA figures can cover a different period. The same is true of ONS's healthy and disability-free life expectancy, which ONS publishes for MSOAs only. So the map says under its key that these LA figures are our estimates. It also leaves the City of London out of their LA rankings, and the Area profile shows OHID's own LA figures. Where our data includes OHID's LA figure for one of these indicators, the map uses it instead.

Figures published only for a whole LA are not offered on the map

Some health figures, such as smoking, obesity and healthy life expectancy from OHID's main profiles, are only published for each whole LA. Every ward in that LA would have the same figure. Ranking wards on it would give an order that looks real but only shows which LA each ward is in. So these indicators are not in the indicator list. Ward profiles and reports still show them, labelled as the LA's figure.

Services are counted where they stand

GP practices, pharmacies, dentists and hospitals are counted in the ward they stand in, which we find from their postcode. A practice that serves people in the next ward still counts only in its own ward. So these counts will not always match a count made another way.

Primary care networks are lists of patients, not areas

A primary care network (PCN) is a group of GP practices. Its population is the people registered with those practices, wherever they live. The list of which practices belong to which network comes from OHID, which follows NHS England's list. Where their patients live comes from NHS Digital's Patients Registered at a GP Practice by LSOA. When those figures are available, choosing a network shades every LSOA with five or more of its patients. The shade shows the share of that LSOA's registered residents who use the network. This means overlapping catchments (the areas a network's patients come from) show as they really are. The PCN footprints outline gives each LSOA to the network that most of its registered residents use. This makes a handy boundary, but NHS England does not publish it and practices do not work to it.

What the map covers

The map covers all of England. Each of the nine English regions is built separately, from the same sources and in the same way. You can switch between them on the map: London, the North East, the North West, Yorkshire and The Humber, the East Midlands, the West Midlands, the East of England, the South East and the South West.

The England view

The England view puts all nine regions together on one map, at every level. A few things are different from a single region. Boundaries are simplified a little more, so that about 7,000 wards can still be shown in a browser. This moves a ward, LSOA or MSOA edge by no more than about 25 metres, and an LA outline by no more than about 100 metres. An indicator that only one region has is left out rather than shown as a mostly empty map. The charities directory stays with each region, because for the whole country it is too big to open at once.

How figures are placed onto areas

Figures come from many publishers, for different years and different kinds of area. Boundaries also change: wards are redrawn, councils merge, and the small Census areas were updated in 2021. This section explains how each figure reaches a ward or an LA, so that every step can be checked. The exact files are listed in the technical appendix.

Most figures are combined using ONS lookups

Most figures are published for small areas: output areas, LSOAs or MSOAs. We combine them into wards using lookup files published by ONS, which list the ward each small area belongs to. Every LSOA is placed in one ward using the ONS best-fit lookup for the wards in force from May 2025. We never split an LSOA. If it crosses a ward boundary, the whole LSOA goes to the ward where most of its residents live. Census figures use the ONS output area lookup instead, because output areas are smaller and follow ward boundaries more closely. If that lookup cannot be used, Census figures use the LSOA lookup too. MSOA figures reach wards through their LSOAs: each LSOA takes its MSOA's figure and is then placed in its ward in the usual way. Every LSOA and every ward sits wholly inside one LA.

Some things are placed by where they are

Services, crimes and a few other figures do not go through a lookup. GP practices, pharmacies, dentists, hospitals and charities are placed by postcode, in the ward and LSOA the ONS Postcode Directory gives that postcode. Crimes are placed in the ward and LSOA whose boundary contains the map position police.uk gives them. So a crime or a practice is counted in the ward it is really in, which is not always the ward its LSOA is given to. This is true of about 1 in 10 of them. Air pollution comes on a 1 km grid, and each LSOA takes the average of the grid squares it covers, by area. Distances to stations and bus stops are measured in a straight line from the middle of each LSOA. Distances to the nearest GP practice, pharmacy and NHS dentist are measured in a straight line from the point ONS places where most of each LSOA's people live (its population-weighted centre), to the nearest site anywhere in England. Care homes are placed by postcode, and road casualties in the LSOA the casualty lives in, as DfT records it. Food businesses on the FSA register are placed in the LSOA whose boundary contains the map position FSA gives them, or by postcode where it gives none. Charities are linked to the LAs they say they work in by matching the LA names in the Charity Commission register. PCN footprints come from where each network's registered patients live (see Primary care networks are lists of patients, not areas).

Adding up and averaging

When LSOAs are combined into a ward, counts such as population and the claimant count are added up. Rates, percentages and scores are weighted by population, which means each LSOA counts in proportion to the number of people living there at the 2021 Census. Fuel poverty is a share of households, so it is weighted by the number of households instead, and so is household income, which is an average over households. Income deprivation affecting children is weighted by the children aged 0 to 15 that MHCLG counts in each LSOA, and income deprivation affecting older people by the people aged 60 and over, so each is the share of the area's children or older people. Census percentages are worked out from the counts behind them.Census percentages are averaged from their output areas, weighted by residents. Counts of stations within 1 km and bus stops within 800 metres are averaged, not added, because one station is close to several LSOAs. Figures published for MSOAs, from Local Health and from ONS, reach wards through their LSOAs: each LSOA takes its MSOA's figure, and the ward's figure is the average of its LSOAs, weighted by residents, or by households for household income. For Local Health figures, a ward has no figure unless the LSOAs with figures hold at least four in five of its residents. The same rule applies to ONS's MSOA figures, counted in households for income. Where no LA figure is published, we work out the LA's figure from its wards in the same way. Fuel poverty, and the deprivation measures beyond the IMD's seven domains, are worked out from all of the LA's LSOAs instead. Household income is worked out from all of the LA's MSOAs, which sit wholly inside it, weighted by households. Census figures are worked out as explained under Census figures at every level.

A few figures are rates made from counts we add up ourselves. For the claimant rate aged 18 to 24, residents killed or seriously injured on the roads, and care home beds per 100 people aged 75 and over, we add up the counts of the LSOAs in each ward, and the people they are counted out of, before dividing. LA figures add up all the LSOAs in the LA in the same way. These are shown for wards and LAs only, because an LSOA has too few claimants, casualties or care homes for its own figure to mean much. The mid-year population estimates are added up into wards and LAs in the same way, and the distances to GP practices, pharmacies and dentists are averaged, weighted by population.

The DWP benefit figures from Stat-Xplore are made the same way: DWP's counts for each LSOA are added up for each ward and LA, and so are the people they are counted out of, before dividing. Universal Credit is also shown for LSOAs, because its counts there are in the tens to hundreds. Personal Independence Payment, Disability Living Allowance, Attendance Allowance, Carer's Allowance and Pension Credit are shown for wards and LAs only, because their counts for an LSOA are small. DWP rounds or adjusts small counts to protect privacy, so an LSOA whose count it withholds is left out with its base, and an area missing more than a fifth of its base has no figure. Where DWP publishes a benefit on the LSOAs of 2011, each count is moved onto the LSOAs of 2021 through the ONS lookup, a split LSOA's count shared among its parts by residents. Children in low income families are published by DWP for wards, and are used only where DWP's wards are the 2025 wards the map draws.

The healthy places index (AHAH) and its drive times, and the mental health index (SAMHI), are averaged into wards and LAs weighted by population. Their deciles, and those of the risk of food insecurity (PPFI), stay with LSOAs, because an average of deciles is not a decile. For wards and LAs we show instead the share of residents who live in an LSOA in the fifth of England at highest risk of food insecurity. The takeaways, pubs and food hygiene ratings are counted for each LSOA and added up into wards and LAs before dividing, and a ward with fewer than 20 rated food businesses has no hygiene figure. The estimates of illness recorded by GPs are published for MSOAs, and reach wards and LAs through their LSOAs as the other MSOA figures do, weighted by residents.

Best fit, not exact fit

The ONS lookups give each LSOA to the one ward it mostly sits in, based on where people live. Many LSOAs cross a ward boundary (see LSOAs do not sit inside wards). So a ward figure built this way is a close estimate for the ward, not an exact count. But every figure built from LSOAs uses the same lookup, so ward figures can still be fairly compared with each other. For a few wards, the LSOAs given to them hold a lot more or a lot fewer people than the ward itself. Where the LSOAs hold under 80% or over 125% of the ward's residents, the ward is marked in the Area profile and the ward report.

Census figures at every level

ONS publishes Census 2021 figures for output areas, LSOAs and MSOAs. At those levels the map shows ONS's own figure, so no matching is needed. For wards, we work out Census figures from output areas rather than LSOAs, using ONS's output area to ward best fit lookup. This is because output areas are about a fifth the size of LSOAs and follow ward boundaries much more closely. Each ward percentage is an average of the percentages of the output areas in the ward, weighted by their residents. So a measure of households, such as the share with no car or van, is weighted by residents too.Each ward percentage is calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in the ward. For example, we take the households with no car or van in those output areas and divide by all their households. So a measure of households is a share of the ward's households, and a measure of people is a share of its residents. LA figures are an average of the ward figures, weighted by population.LA figures are calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in each LA's wards.

To see output areas, choose a Census indicator and OA on the level switch. Their borders appear as you zoom in. An output area holds about 125 households, so a single household can change a percentage quite a lot. These figures are best read as a picture of a street or estate, not an exact measure.

MSOAs

MSOAs have their own place on the level switch. An MSOA is made of whole LSOAs. But many LSOAs cross a ward boundary (see LSOAs do not sit inside wards), so MSOAs and wards do not fit inside one another. Where an MSOA edge matches a ward edge, it is by chance. Local Health figures are published for MSOAs, and so are ONS's household income estimates and its healthy and disability-free life expectancy. All of them reach wards through the LSOAs the two share.

Figures estimated from 2011 areas are marked

Some Local Health figures are still published for the 2011 MSOAs. Where a 2011 area was split or merged to make a 2021 area, the figure for the 2021 area is worked out from the 2011 ones, weighted by how many people lived in each. The map then shows Estimated from 2011 areas next to it. It does not compare the figure with England, because the publisher's comparison was for the old area. At the moment, OHID publishes no figure at all for the areas that changed. So those areas are left blank, and nothing on the map is estimated in this way.

LA codes are checked at every update

Each LA has an official code. At every update we check that each LA on the map was found under its current code in the publisher's figures. Without this, an LA that had merged or changed its code since the figures were published could go missing without anyone noticing. Any LA that does not match is flagged so that we can put it right.

Known gaps

City of London

The City of London has 25 wards, but its few LSOAs are each matched to just five of them. So the other 20 wards have no deprivation score, no Local Health figures and none of the other figures built from LSOAs. Census figures are worked out from output areas instead, so some of those 20 wards do have them. This comes from the way the areas are drawn, not from a missed update. Choose LA to see the figures for the City as a whole.

MSOAs redrawn in 2021

The older Local Health figures, such as hospital admissions for 2016/17 to 2020/21, are published for the 2011 MSOAs. OHID leaves out the MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021. That is about 2% of England's MSOAs and 39 of London's. These areas are blank for those figures rather than estimated. The map's notice and the area's hover card give this as the reason, not privacy.

The newer figures, such as deaths and life expectancy for 2019 to 2023, are published for the 2021 MSOAs, so boundary changes leave no gaps in them. OHID still holds back some MSOA figures where the numbers are small, for example men's life expectancy for 52 of London's MSOAs. Where that leaves a ward with figures for fewer than four in five of its residents, we show no figure for the ward (see Missing is not zero). When a ward figure covers most but not all of its residents, it says what share it covers.

Charities are listed region by region

The charities directory is not part of the England view. Open a region to see it.

PCN catchments are not always available

The figures for where each network's patients live come from an NHS Digital file. It cannot be downloaded automatically, so we add it by hand at each update. Until the latest file has been added, a network shows its practices but no catchment.

Benefits other than the claimant count

Current figures for Universal Credit, Personal Independence Payment, Disability Living Allowance, Attendance Allowance, Carer's Allowance, Pension Credit and children in low income families by small area are published only through DWP's Stat-Xplore service, which needs an account key. Nomis has either never carried them or stops in 2018 on older boundaries. The map reads them from Stat-Xplore once its key has been set up, and until a data refresh has brought them in they are left out of every list. Employment and Support Allowance is not included. The claimant count, which covers people claiming benefits because they are unemployed, is included.
