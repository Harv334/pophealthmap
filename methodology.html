Methodology and technical notes

PopHealth Map shows more than 100 indicators on one map of England. An indicator is a measure that you can colour the map by, such as life expectancy. The figures come from the Census, the National Health Service (NHS), the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) and other official sources. This page explains where each figure comes from, and how we calculate it for wards and neighbourhoods. It also explains what a figure can tell you, and what it cannot tell you. If you have little time, read the 5 things to know at the start of How to read the figures. The technical appendix at the end lists the exact files that we use from the Office for National Statistics (ONS).

PopHealth Map is in beta, which means that it is new and we are still testing it. If a figure looks wrong, please use Report a problem on its card, or email contact@pophealth.uk. We will check it against the publisher's own figures.

How to cite this map

When you use a figure from this map, please name PopHealth Map and also the organisation that published the figure. The publisher's own release is the official source for that figure. Each indicator's card gives its source, the period it covers and the level of area that it is published for. We suggest that you name the source like this:

PopHealth Map, indicator for area, period. Figures from publisher, updated on the date shown at the bottom of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on date.

For example, this is how to cite one of the Local Health figures:

PopHealth Map, Life expectancy at birth, women, for the wards of Tower Hamlets, 2019 to 2023, updated on the date shown at the bottom of the map. https://pophealth.uk, accessed on the day you looked. Ward estimates calculated by PopHealth Map from Office for Health Improvement and Disparities (OHID) Local Health figures for middle layer super output areas (MSOAs).

To show someone exactly what you saw, use Copy link at the top of the map. The link keeps the indicator, the area and the level that you chose. Most of the figures are published under the Open Government Licence. This licence lets you use them again if you say where they came from. Licences and credits gives the wording that each publisher asks for.

How to read the figures

5 things to know before you use a figure

  • A darker colour usually means a higher figure, whether a high figure is good or bad. For deprivation, a darker colour always means more deprived, even where a lower number means more deprived. The 2 ends of the legend (the colour key) are not the highest and lowest areas. The low end has 5% of areas below it, and the high end has 5% of areas above it.
  • Each indicator has its own period, and some periods are several years old. The date at the bottom of the map is when we last updated the figures. It is not the period that the figures describe.
  • A blank area has no figure. That is not the same as a figure of zero.
  • Each indicator is published for one level of area, and we calculate the other levels from it. So a ward figure is usually a close estimate, not an exact count. Each indicator's card says which level its publisher uses.
  • When 2 indicators increase and decrease together, that is a reason to look more carefully. It does not prove that one causes the other.

None of these points is a mistake. Each one comes from the way that the figures are published, and the sections below explain them. The map shows figures for several types of area. The local authority (LA) is the area of one council, and each LA is divided into wards. The map also uses MSOAs, which are areas of about 8,000 people, and LSOAs, which are small areas of about 1,500 people. The smallest areas are output areas, with about 125 households each. Words used on this map explains each type of area.

What a figure measures

Every indicator has a card

To open the card for an indicator, select the About this indicator button under the chosen indicator. You can also select the key at the bottom of the map. The card says what is counted, and out of what total. It gives the level of area that the publisher uses, and how we calculate the other levels. It also gives the period, the source and how often the source is updated. The Local Health figures come from the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities (OHID). For these figures, the card uses OHID's own description. This includes the unit, whether the years are calendar years or financial years, and when OHID last updated the figure.

Each indicator has its own period

The period next to a legend is the period that the publisher gives for that indicator. It can be very different from one indicator to the next. The date at the bottom of the map is when we last updated the figures, not the period that they describe. Each update uses the newest figures published, but some of these figures describe years long before the update.

Older figures, and the Census during lockdown

If an indicator's period ended more than 3 years ago, the indicator list and the legend show its end year. This shows clearly that a figure for 2016/17 to 2020/21 is not current. (2016/17 means the financial year from April 2016 to March 2017.) Census 2021 indicators are not marked, because Census 2021 is the latest Census. But its figures for travel to work have a warning. The Census was on 21 March 2021, during a COVID-19 lockdown. ONS says that those figures do not show normal travel patterns.

Several Local Health periods include the COVID-19 pandemic. Deaths and life expectancy for 2019 to 2023 include 2020 and 2021. In those years, COVID-19 increased deaths most in the more deprived areas. So the differences between places may be bigger than in a normal year. The hospital admissions figures end in 2020/21, the first year of the pandemic, when emergency admissions fell by a large amount. The cards for these indicators say this.

Life expectancy is shown for women and men separately

Life expectancy at birth is the average number of years that a newborn baby would live. It assumes that death rates stay as they were in the period. Local Health gives it for women and for men, but not for everyone together. So the map shows it as 2 indicators.

What the colours show

Darker means a higher figure

The map colours every indicator from light for its lowest figures to dark for its highest figures. This is true whether a high figure is good or bad. The exception is the deprivation deciles and ranks. Deprivation deciles go from 1 to 10. Decile 1 is the most deprived 10% of areas in England, and decile 10 is the least deprived 10%. Ranks also start at 1 for the most deprived area. So for these indicators we reverse the colours, and a darker colour still means more deprived. The Highest and lowest 10 lists and the printable reports say whether a higher figure is better or worse. For the Local Health figures, they use the direction that OHID gives. Some indicators only describe who lives in an area. Examples are the share of residents over 65, in social housing or in each ethnic group. These have no better or worse end, so the lists call them highest and lowest, not best and worst.

The ends of the legend are not the highest and lowest areas

The colours go from the 5th percentile to the 95th percentile of the areas shown. The 5th percentile is the figure that 5% of areas are below. The 95th percentile is the figure that 5% of areas are above. This means that a single very high or very low area does not use the whole colour scale. An area below the low end has the low end's colour. An area above the high end has the high end's colour. When this happens, the legend marks that end with ≤ (or less) or ≥ (or more). For example, "≥ 37.8" means 37.8 or more. The Highest and lowest 10 lists give the real figures.

How a figure compares with other areas

Hover over an area, or tap it on a phone, to compare its figure with other areas of the same type. It compares the area with the region that you are viewing. For example, it might say "higher than 82% of London's LSOAs". It does not compare the area with all of England, so the result changes if you choose a different region.

How certain a figure is

Missing is not zero

When an area has no figure, the figure is missing. It does not mean zero. There are 2 reasons for this. First, OHID does not publish some MSOA figures, because the numbers are so small that someone could be identified. Second, some older indicators have no figure for the MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021 (see MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021). In Query, an area with no figure for one of your conditions cannot pass or fail that condition. So the area is not in the results, and the number of these areas is shown under the results.

Comparing a figure with the England average

OHID compares each Local Health figure for an MSOA with the England average. The result is "Better than the England average", "Worse than the England average", "Higher than the England average", "Lower than the England average" or "About the same as the England average". Better, worse, higher or lower means the difference is too big to be chance. About the same means the difference could be chance. OHID uses higher or lower for indicators where it does not say whether a higher figure is good or bad. Hover over an MSOA to see OHID's result next to the figure.

The England average is OHID's figure for England as a whole. For example, it is all the emergency admissions in England divided by all the people in England. It is not the middle MSOA (the median), and it is not a simple average of the MSOA figures, so areas with more people count for more in it. For a ratio, the England average is 100.

For this test, OHID uses a confidence interval. This is the range the true figure is very likely to be in (95% likely). If the range does not include England's figure, the difference is too big to be chance. Many MSOA figures are based on small numbers of people. So an area can look high on the colour scale but still be about the same as the England average by this test. Ward and LA figures have no test, because we calculate them from MSOA figures. The few MSOA figures estimated from 2011 areas also have no test.

Modelled figures have a range

Some figures are modelled estimates. A modelled estimate is an estimate from a statistical model, not a count. ONS's household income figures for MSOAs are modelled estimates. So are its healthy life expectancy and disability-free life expectancy for MSOAs. ONS calculates them from surveys and records, because it surveys too few people in each MSOA to measure them directly. ONS gives each MSOA figure a confidence interval, the range the true figure is very likely to be in (95% likely). Hover over an MSOA to see it. The ranges of many neighbouring MSOAs overlap, which means that these MSOAs are not clearly different. Ward and LA figures have no range, because we calculate them from MSOA figures.

How an area gets its figure

How we match figures to areas gives the full method. This section explains the parts of the method that change what you see.

Some LSOAs cross ward boundaries

LSOA boundaries come from the 2021 Census, and the ward boundaries that the map uses are from May 2025. So the two do not always match, and about 4 in 10 LSOAs cross a ward boundary. We never split an LSOA. ONS publishes a lookup, which is a table that says which ward each LSOA belongs to. It is a best fit lookup: ONS places each LSOA in the ward where most of its people live. We use this lookup without changes, and the technical appendix names it. So a ward's figure counts each whole LSOA once. It does not split an LSOA by the land that the LSOA shares with other wards. If you zoom in and see LSOA and ward edges crossing, that is how the boundaries are really drawn. It is not a fault in the map.

How figures are combined into wards and LAs

When we combine small areas into wards or LAs, we add together counts. Examples are population, the number of services and the claimant count. The claimant count is the number of people who claim benefits because they are unemployed. Rates, percentages and scores are weighted by population. This means that we use an average where areas with more people count for more. The number of people in each small area is from the 2021 Census. Fuel poverty is a share of households, so it is weighted by the number of households instead. Household income is an average for households, so it is also weighted by households. Counts of stations within 1 km and bus stops within 800 metres are averaged, not added. This is because one station is close to several LSOAs. So a ward's figure shows what its average resident has nearby. Census percentages are also combined from their output areas by weighting, as explained in Census figures at every level.Census percentages are different, because ONS publishes the counts behind them. We calculate each one from the summed counts of the output areas, as explained in Census figures at every level. This is how LSOA figures become ward figures. MSOA figures become ward figures through the LSOAs that the MSOA and the ward share. So a ward that crosses 2 MSOAs gets a mix of the 2 MSOA figures, in proportion to where its people live.

Some figures are not published for LAs. For these, we calculate each LA's figure from its wards in the same way. This gives the rate for all of the LA's residents. It is not a simple average of the wards, which have different sizes. Fuel poverty is calculated from all of the LA's LSOAs, weighted by households. Household income is calculated from all of the LA's MSOAs, also weighted by households. An LA figure that we calculate can be slightly different from the council's or the publisher's figure. This is because they may use a different population or year. For OHID's Local Health indicators, the difference can be bigger. Life expectancy, and ratios where England is 100, cannot be averaged exactly. Also, OHID's own LA figures can cover a different period. The same is true of ONS's healthy life expectancy and disability-free life expectancy, which ONS publishes for MSOAs only. So for these indicators, the map says under its key that the LA figures are our estimates. The map also does not include the City of London in their LA rankings. The Area profile shows OHID's own LA figures. Where our data has OHID's LA figure for one of these indicators, the map uses OHID's figure.

Figures published only for a whole LA are not in the indicator list

Some health figures from OHID's main profiles are published only for each whole LA. Examples are smoking, obesity and physical activity. With these figures, every ward in an LA would have the same figure. A ranking of wards would look real, but it would only show which LA each ward is in. So these indicators are not in the indicator list. Ward profiles and reports still show them, labelled as the LA's figure.

Services are counted in the ward where they are

We count general practitioner (GP) practices, pharmacies, dentists and hospitals in the ward where they are. We find this ward from their postcode. A practice that serves people in the next ward still counts only in its own ward. So these counts will not always match a count made in a different way.

Primary care networks are lists of patients, not areas

A primary care network (PCN) is a group of GP practices. Its population is the people registered with those practices, wherever they live. OHID gives the list of practices in each network, and OHID's list follows NHS England's list. The figures for where the patients live come from NHS Digital's Patients Registered at a GP Practice, by LSOA. When those figures are available and you choose a network, the map colours every LSOA with 5 or more of its patients. The colour shows the share of that LSOA's registered residents who use the network. A network's catchment is the area that its patients come from. The map shows catchments that overlap as they really are. The PCN footprints outline gives each LSOA to the network that most of its registered residents use. This gives a useful boundary, but NHS England does not publish it, and practices do not use it in their work.

What the map covers

The map covers all of England. We make the map for each of the 9 English regions separately, from the same sources and in the same way. You can change between the regions on the map. They are London, the North East, the North West, Yorkshire and The Humber, the East Midlands, the West Midlands, the East of England, the South East and the South West.

The England view

The England view shows all 9 regions together on one map, at every level. A few things are different from the view of a single region. Boundaries are a little simpler, so that a browser can still show about 7,000 wards. This moves a ward, LSOA or MSOA edge by about 25 metres or less. It moves an LA outline by about 100 metres or less. An indicator that only one region has is not in the England view, because its map would be mostly empty. The charities directory is only in the view of each region. The directory for the whole country is too big to open at once.

How we match figures to areas

Figures come from many publishers, for different years and different types of area. Boundaries also change: ward boundaries move, councils join together, and the small Census areas changed in 2021. This section explains how we calculate the figure for each ward and LA, so that you can check every step. The technical appendix lists the exact files.

Most figures are combined using ONS lookups

Most figures are published for small areas: output areas, LSOAs or MSOAs. We combine them into wards with lookup files from ONS. A lookup file lists the ward that each small area belongs to. We place every LSOA in one ward with the ONS best fit lookup for the ward boundaries of May 2025. This means that ONS places each LSOA in the ward where most of its people live. We never split an LSOA, even if it crosses a ward boundary. Census figures use the ONS output area lookup instead, because output areas are smaller and follow ward boundaries more closely. If we cannot use that lookup, Census figures also use the LSOA lookup. MSOA figures reach wards through their LSOAs. Each LSOA takes the figure of its MSOA, and then goes to its ward in the usual way. Every LSOA and every ward is completely inside one LA.

Some things are placed by their location

Services, crimes and a few other figures do not use a lookup. GP practices, pharmacies, dentists, hospitals and charities are placed by postcode. Each one goes in the ward and LSOA that the ONS Postcode Directory gives its postcode. Crimes are placed in the ward and LSOA whose boundary contains the map position police.uk gives them. So a crime or a practice is counted in the ward where it really is. For about 1 in 10 of them, this is not the ward that their LSOA belongs to. Air pollution figures are for the squares of a 1 km grid. Each LSOA takes the average of the grid squares that it covers, weighted by area. Distances to stations and bus stops are measured in a straight line from the middle of each LSOA. Distances to the nearest GP practice, pharmacy and NHS dentist are measured in a straight line to the nearest one anywhere in England. We measure them from the point that ONS places where most of each LSOA's people live (its population-weighted centre). Care homes are placed by postcode. Road casualties are placed in the LSOA where the casualty lives, as DfT records it. Food businesses on the FSA register are placed in the LSOA whose boundary contains the map position that FSA gives them. If FSA gives no position, they are placed by postcode. Charities are linked to the LAs where they say they work, by matching the LA names in the Charity Commission register. PCN footprints come from where each network's registered patients live (see Primary care networks are lists of patients, not areas).

Totals and averages

When we combine LSOAs into a ward, we add together counts such as population and the claimant count. Rates, percentages and scores are weighted by population. This means that each LSOA counts in proportion to the number of people who lived there at the 2021 Census. Fuel poverty is a share of households, so it is weighted by the number of households instead. Household income is an average for households, so it is also weighted by households. Income deprivation affecting children is weighted by the number of children aged 0 to 15 that MHCLG counts in each LSOA. Income deprivation affecting older people is weighted by the number of people aged 60 and over. So each one is the share of the area's children or older people. Census percentages are calculated from the counts behind them.Census percentages are averaged from their output areas, weighted by residents. Counts of stations within 1 km and bus stops within 800 metres are averaged, not added. This is because one station is close to several LSOAs. Figures published for MSOAs, from Local Health and from ONS, reach wards through their LSOAs. Each LSOA takes the figure of its MSOA. The ward's figure is the average of its LSOAs, weighted by residents, or by households for household income. For Local Health figures, a ward has no figure unless the LSOAs with figures have at least 4 in 5 of its residents. The same rule applies to ONS's MSOA figures, but for income it counts households. Where no LA figure is published, we calculate the LA's figure from its wards in the same way. Fuel poverty is calculated from all of the LA's LSOAs instead. So are the deprivation measures that are not one of the IMD's 7 domains. Household income is calculated from all of the LA's MSOAs, weighted by households. Each MSOA is completely inside one LA. Census figures are calculated as explained in Census figures at every level.

We calculate a few rates ourselves from counts that we add together. These are the claimant rate for people aged 18 to 24, residents killed or seriously injured on the roads, and care home beds per 100 people aged 75 and over. For each ward, we add together the counts of its LSOAs. We also add together the numbers of people that the counts are out of, and then we divide. LA figures use all the LSOAs in the LA in the same way. These rates are shown for wards and LAs only. This is because an LSOA has too few claimants, casualties or care homes for its own figure to be useful. The mid-year population estimates are added together into wards and LAs in the same way. The distances to GP practices, pharmacies and dentists are averaged, weighted by population.

We calculate the DWP benefit figures from Stat-Xplore in the same way. For each ward and LA, we add together DWP's counts for its LSOAs. We also add together the numbers of people that the counts are out of, and then we divide. Universal Credit is also shown for LSOAs, because its counts for an LSOA are tens or hundreds of people. Other benefits are shown for wards and LAs only, because their counts for an LSOA are small. These are Personal Independence Payment, Disability Living Allowance, Attendance Allowance, Carer's Allowance and Pension Credit. DWP rounds or changes small counts to protect privacy. If DWP does not publish the count for an LSOA, we do not include that LSOA or its base. The base is the number of people that the count is out of. An area that is missing more than 20% of its base has no figure. Some benefits are published for the 2011 LSOAs. For these, we move each count onto the 2021 LSOAs with the ONS lookup. When a 2011 LSOA was split, we share its count between its parts by the number of residents. DWP publishes children in low income families for wards. We use these figures only where DWP's wards are the 2025 wards that the map shows.

The healthy places index (AHAH) and its drive times are averaged into wards and LAs, weighted by population. So is the mental health index (SAMHI). Their deciles are for LSOAs only, because an average of deciles is not a decile. The same is true of the deciles of the risk of food insecurity (PPFI). For food insecurity in wards and LAs, we show the share of residents in the LSOAs at highest risk. These are the 20% of LSOAs in England with the highest risk. We count takeaways, pubs and food hygiene ratings for each LSOA. We add these counts together into wards and LAs, and then divide. A ward with fewer than 20 rated food businesses has no hygiene figure. The estimates of illness recorded by GPs are published for MSOAs. Like the other MSOA figures, they reach wards and LAs through their LSOAs, weighted by residents.

Best fit, not exact fit

The ONS lookups place each LSOA in the ward where most of its people live. Many LSOAs cross a ward boundary (see Some LSOAs cross ward boundaries). So a ward figure calculated in this way is a close estimate for the ward, not an exact count. But every figure calculated from LSOAs uses the same lookup, so you can still fairly compare ward figures with each other. For a few wards, the LSOAs placed in the ward have many more or many fewer people than the ward. Sometimes these LSOAs have less than 80% or more than 125% of the ward's residents. The Area profile and the ward report then mark the ward.

A few wards are smaller than the LSOAs around them, so the lookup places no LSOA in them. Most of these wards are in the City of London. For these wards, we use output areas. Each output area is inside one LSOA, and ONS's output area lookup places each output area in a ward. So we can find the LSOA where most of the ward's residents live. The ward takes that LSOA's figures as published, including the MSOA figures that the LSOA has. Nothing is averaged. Counts, such as the claimant count, are blank for these wards. This is because that LSOA's counts already belong to the ward that the lookup places it in. These wards are marked in the same way, and the mark says which LSOA their figures come from.

Census figures at every level

ONS publishes Census 2021 figures for output areas, LSOAs and MSOAs. At those levels, the map shows ONS's own figure, so no matching is necessary. For wards, we calculate Census figures from output areas, not from LSOAs. We use ONS's best fit lookup from output areas to wards. This is because each LSOA has about 5 output areas, and output areas follow ward boundaries much more closely. Each ward percentage is an average of the percentages of the output areas in the ward, weighted by their residents. So a measure of households, such as the share with no car or van, is also weighted by residents.Each ward percentage is calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in the ward. For example, we take the households with no car or van in those output areas, and divide by all their households. So a measure of households is a share of the ward's households, and a measure of people is a share of its residents. LA figures are an average of the ward figures, weighted by population.LA figures are calculated from the summed counts of the output areas in each LA's wards.

To see output areas, choose a Census indicator, and then choose OA (output area) on the level switch. The borders of output areas appear when you zoom in. An output area has about 125 households, so a single household can change a percentage by a large amount. Use these figures as a picture of a street or estate, not as an exact measure.

MSOAs

MSOAs have their own place on the level switch. An MSOA is made of whole LSOAs. But many LSOAs cross a ward boundary (see Some LSOAs cross ward boundaries). So an MSOA is not always inside one ward, and a ward is not always inside one MSOA. Where an MSOA edge matches a ward edge, this is by chance. Local Health figures are published for MSOAs. So are ONS's household income estimates, and its healthy life expectancy and disability-free life expectancy. All of these figures reach wards through the LSOAs that the MSOA and the ward share.

Figures estimated from 2011 areas are marked

Some Local Health figures are published for the 2011 MSOAs. Some 2011 areas were split or joined together to make 2021 areas. For these, we calculate the figure for the 2021 area from the 2011 areas, weighted by how many people lived in each. The map then shows Estimated from 2011 areas next to the figure. It does not compare the figure with the England average, because the publisher's comparison was for the old area. But OHID publishes no figure for the areas that changed. So those areas are blank, and no figure on the map is estimated in this way.

LA codes are checked at every update

Each LA has an official code. At every update, we check that we found each LA on the map under its current code in the publisher's figures. Without this check, an LA could be missing and nobody would notice. This could happen if the LA joined with another LA, or changed its code, after the figures were published. If an LA does not match, we mark it so that we can correct it.

Known gaps

City of London

The City of London has 25 wards but only 6 LSOAs. 10 of its wards have residents in ONS's output area lookup, and all 10 have a deprivation score and the other figures calculated from LSOAs. Some of them take these figures from the LSOA where most of their residents live (see wards with no LSOA). The other 15 wards have no output area of their own, because very few people live in them, so they have no figures about residents. One of them, Walbrook, has an LSOA in the lookup, so it has a deprivation score but no Census figures. This is because of the way that the areas are drawn. It is not because we missed an update. Choose LA to see the figures for the whole City of London.

MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021

The older Local Health figures, such as hospital admissions for 2016/17 to 2020/21, are published for the 2011 MSOAs. OHID does not publish these figures for the MSOAs whose boundaries changed in 2021. These are about 2% of England's MSOAs, and 39 of London's MSOAs. For those figures, these areas are blank, not estimated. The map's notice and the area's hover card say that the figure is missing because the boundaries changed, not for privacy.

The newer figures, such as deaths and life expectancy for 2019 to 2023, are published for the 2021 MSOAs. So boundary changes cause no gaps in them. But OHID does not publish some MSOA figures, because the numbers are too small. For example, it does not publish men's life expectancy for 52 of London's MSOAs. If a ward then has figures for fewer than 4 in 5 of its residents, we show no figure for the ward (see Missing is not zero). When a ward figure covers most of its residents but not all, it says what share it covers.

Charities are shown for one region at a time

The charities directory is not part of the England view. Open a region to see it.

PCN catchments are not always available

The figures for where each network's patients live come from an NHS Digital file. We cannot download this file automatically, so we add it by hand at each update. Before we add the latest file, a network shows its practices but no catchment.

Benefits other than the claimant count

DWP publishes current figures for small areas for several benefits, and for children in low income families. The benefits are Universal Credit, Personal Independence Payment, Disability Living Allowance, Attendance Allowance, Carer's Allowance and Pension Credit. DWP publishes these figures only through its Stat-Xplore service, which needs an account key. Nomis does not have these figures, or its figures stop in 2018 and use older boundaries. The map gets these figures from Stat-Xplore with an account key. If no update has got them yet, they are not in any list. Employment and Support Allowance is not included. The claimant count, which counts people who claim benefits because they are unemployed, is included.
