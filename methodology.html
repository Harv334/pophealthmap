Methodology and technical notes

← Back to the map · PopHealth Map, London

This page records where each figure comes from, which geography and year it belongs to, and what it cannot tell you. It is written to be read alongside the map rather than instead of it.

This tool is in development. The data is real and comes from published sources, but the map is still being built and checked, so treat figures as provisional.

Geographies, and which year each one is from

The four geographies on the map come from different censuses and boundary reviews. They do not nest inside one another, and that has consequences worth understanding before comparing them.

MSOAs can now be drawn as well as aggregated through. They are a layer of their own in the sidebar rather than a third position on the ward and LSOA switch, because they nest into neither: an MSOA is built from whole LSOAs, but 41% of LSOAs cross a ward line, so an MSOA edge agreeing with a ward edge is a coincidence rather than a rule. The layer is fetched only when you switch it on. An MSOA is in scope when at least one of its LSOAs is, which is the same definition the rest of the pipeline works to.

All boundary layers are the BGC generalisation: simplified to 20 metres and clipped to the coastline. Keeping them at the same generalisation matters. LSOAs were previously taken at BSC, 200 metres, which is ten times coarser, and because the two had been simplified independently their shared borders no longer coincided: LSOA edges visibly cut across ward lines.

Coordinates in the files the browser downloads are rounded to five decimal places, which moves no point by more than 0.32 metres and roughly halves the file size.

LSOAs do not sit inside wards

LSOA boundaries are from 2021 and wards were redrawn in 2025, so the two genuinely cross. Measured across the whole of London, the typical LSOA falls 0.7% outside the ward it is assigned to, and 59% sit entirely within one, but 41% cross a ward line somewhere and a small number straddle almost completely.

Every LSOA is assigned to exactly one ward using the ONS best-fit lookup (LSOA21_WD25_LAD25_EW_LU_v2). So a ward figure built from LSOAs counts each LSOA once, in the ward it best fits, not in proportion to the area it shares. At high zoom you will see LSOA outlines crossing ward outlines. That is the geography being honest, not a drawing error.

How figures move between levels

DirectionMethod
LSOA → ward Population-weighted mean for rates, scores and percentages. Sum for counts.
MSOA → ward Through the LSOAs the two share. Each LSOA carries its MSOA's value and contributes its population as weight, so a ward straddling two MSOAs gets a blend in proportion to where its people are.
Ward → borough Population-weighted mean, except for genuinely additive measures (population, crime totals, service counts), which are summed.

Only counts are added up. Everything else is averaged, on the principle that a wrongly averaged count looks implausible and gets noticed, while a wrongly summed rate just looks like a number.

Deprivation deciles are not a decile once averaged

IMD deciles run 1 (the most deprived tenth nationally) to 10 (the least). A ward contains several LSOAs, each with its own decile, so the ward figure is a population-weighted mean of deciles and is published as imd_decile_mean rather than pretending to be a decile. A value of 3 means the typical LSOA there sits in the third decile, among roughly the 30% most deprived. It does not mean the area is in the most deprived tenth.

The scores run the other way: a higher imd_score means more deprived. Deprivation is the easiest thing on this map to state backwards.

Health indicators: two sets at two levels

The health data comes from OHID Fingertips in two separate pulls, and they are not interchangeable.

The borough set is the one to be careful with. Ranking wards by it produces a real-looking ordering that is an artefact of the geography rather than a finding about the wards, because every ward in a borough carries the same number. The map and the question panel both flag these.

Fingertips still publishes the Local Health set against 2011 MSOA codes, and 39 London MSOAs were renumbered in 2021. Where a code no longer matches, the value is left missing rather than filled from a neighbour.

Known gaps

  • City of London. Its 20 wards have no deprivation score and no Local Health indicators. They are tiny and share very few LSOAs between them, so there is nothing to aggregate. This is a property of the geography, not a fetch failure.
  • Five layers cover North West London only: schools, community centres, libraries, ESOL providers and CICs. They are badged NWL on the map. Blank elsewhere means not yet collected, not none.
  • Suppressed values. Fingertips withholds MSOA figures where small counts could identify individuals. A ward with no fill may be suppressed rather than zero.

Query: how a filter is evaluated

Query mode asks for wards meeting several conditions at once. Everything runs in your browser against the data already on the page, so nothing is uploaded and no request is made while you build a filter.

A ward matches when it satisfies every part: it contains at least one of each service ticked, it sits in the borough chosen, and it meets all of the indicator conditions. Ticking no services places no service condition at all, rather than requiring a ward to have none.

Where the service counts come from

ServiceCounted by
Pharmacies The pipeline's own pharmacy_count ward indicator
Schools, libraries, community centres The ward code the source file already carries
GP practices, hospitals, dental practices, ESOL providers, CICs A spatial test of the point against the ward polygon

These will not always agree with a count made a different way. A point sitting just inside a boundary is attributed by where it falls, and a service used by a neighbouring ward is still counted in the ward it stands in. Where the pipeline already publishes a count, that figure is used rather than a second one being derived, so the query and the ward profile cannot disagree about the same ward.

Missing is not zero

A ward with no figure for one of your conditions cannot pass it and cannot fail it, so it is left out of the results and the number left out is reported underneath them. A filter that silently discarded the areas it could not judge would hand back a shorter list as though it were the whole answer.

Conditions on the borough-level Fingertips set are flagged as you build them. Filtering wards on a figure that is identical for every ward in a borough selects whole boroughs, and the panel says so rather than letting the result imply a distinction that is not in the data.

Compare: what the colours claim

Compare pins up to four wards and lines them up on the metrics you choose. On each row the highest and lowest of the pinned values are marked with a triangle, and coloured green for the better end and red for the worse.

The colour follows the indicator, not the size of the number. The highest IMD decile is the least deprived tenth and the highest life expectancy is the best outcome, so colouring the largest value red would state the opposite of the truth on those rows. For the Local Health set the direction is OHID's own published RAG polarity, carried through from the source. For the deprivation family it follows the scale: scores rise with deprivation, deciles fall with it.

Where an indicator has no better or worse end, the row is marked but left uncoloured, and the key names which rows those are. A high share of residents over 65, or in social renting, or of any ethnic group, is a fact about who lives there rather than a good or bad result, and the map does not colour it as one.

The question panel

The model is never given the dataset. It is given four tools, and your browser runs them against the JSON already loaded on the page, so every figure in an answer is the same number the map is drawing, nothing is uploaded, and the model cannot invent a statistic because it has none in its context.

It can still describe a correct figure incorrectly, which is why the panel carries a standing note asking you to verify anything you intend to act on.

Questions are capped at 10 a day per person because the service has running costs. The count is enforced by the server against your IP address and resets at midnight UTC; the panel shows what you have left beside its title. The map itself, including Query and Compare, is unlimited: those run entirely in your browser and cost nothing to serve.

Rebuilding this

The whole map is rebuilt by one script, fetch_all_data.py, with no manual downloads. Sources, licences and per-source notes are in the repository: github.com/Harv334/pophealthmap.
