Accessibility statement

This statement is for pophealth.uk, an interactive map of population health across England. You can look at it by region, local authority (LA), ward and neighbourhood. The statement covers the map itself and the pages around it: the Methodology page, the About and privacy page and this statement.

How accessible this site is

You should be able to use most of this site with a keyboard and with a screen reader, which is software that reads the screen out loud. The parts you cannot use are listed below, each with something you can use instead.

These things work:

  • You can zoom every page to 200% without losing any content or features.
  • There is a colour-blind safe set of colours for the map. Turn it on with Colour-blind palette under More at the top of the page.
  • Colour is never the only way a figure is shown. Everything coloured on the map is also written as a number in the side panel and in the downloads.
  • You can reach almost every control with the keyboard, and the one you are on is outlined. The exceptions are listed below.
  • The row of tools (Explore, Query, Compare, Custom area, Data export and Directory) and the tabs at the top of the side panel each take one press of the Tab key. The arrow keys then move between the items inside them.
  • A screen reader tells you when you select a ward, focus on an LA or an integrated care board (ICB), or zoom to an area.
  • Every page has a skip link that takes you straight to the main content.
  • Every indicator has a written card, opened with About this indicator. It explains what is counted, the level of area it is published for, its period, its source and its method.
  • When you point at or tap an area, a small label gives words beside the figure. For example, it says where the area ranks ("higher than 82% of London's LSOAs") and whether a Local Health figure is clearly different from England ("Worse than England, statistically"). So you never have to rely on colour alone.
  • The two-indicator map uses colour to combine two figures. But both figures are written out when you point at or tap an area, and the key explains each colour in words.

What is not accessible

You cannot reach the map's areas with a keyboard. The shapes on the map, such as wards, MSOAs and LSOAs, only respond to a mouse or touch. Hundreds of boundaries appear on screen at once. If each one were a keyboard stop, the rest of the page would be hundreds of key presses away.

Instead, you can get all of the map's data from these tools, which all work with a keyboard:

  • The search box at the top finds an LA, an ICB, a ward, an LSOA, a GP practice, a dental practice, a pharmacy, a hospital or a charity by name, or a place by its postcode. Choosing a ward or an LSOA does exactly the same as selecting it on the map. Choosing an LA or an ICB focuses the map on it.
  • Query lists the wards that match the conditions you set. Each ward in the list is a button that selects it on the map.
  • The Directory has every service and organisation on the map in one list that you can search and filter.
  • Data export saves the figures for every area to a spreadsheet, so nothing is only on the map.

These parts also fall short:

Building a custom area needs a mouse or a touch screen
You build a custom area by selecting LSOAs on the map, so you cannot build one with a keyboard. Once it is built, you can share it as a link that anyone can open. Data export gives the same figures for published areas without using the map.
The site has one light colour scheme
The map and the pages around it, including this statement, use one light colour scheme. They do not change if your device is set to dark.
The map's own background is not described
Street names, rivers and parks come from OpenStreetMap. They are drawn as pictures with no text version, but nothing this site publishes depends on reading them.
Shading is described in words, not read out area by area
When the map is coloured by an indicator, a screen reader is told what the colours mean and what the range is. It will not read out every area. To get them as an ordered list, use Query, the lists of the 10 highest and lowest areas, or Data export.
Some charts are pictures of their data
A few small bar and distribution charts are drawn as pictures rather than written out. But every figure in them appears as a number nearby or in the ward's full profile.
The printable ward and LSOA reports may not be fully accessible
The reports open as a separate page that you can print or save as a PDF. Their charts may not have full text versions. A PDF saved from your browser's print window may also lack the structure a screen reader needs. So the same figures are also in the area's profile on the map and in Data export.
Answers in the Ask panel are written by AI
The Ask about this data panel, on the London map and each region's map, answers your questions. The answers are written by an AI model. They can be wrong or leave things out, so check any figure against the area's profile or the downloads before you rely on it. We have not checked how the panel works with a screen reader. On the England map, which shows every region at once, it is not shown, and nothing is sent.

Reporting accessibility problems

If you find a problem that is not listed here, or you need something on this site in a different format, email contact@pophealth.uk. Tell us which page it was on and what you were trying to do. We aim to reply within five working days.

If you ask for information in an accessible format and you are not happy with our reply, tell us when you write back and we will look at it again.

Technical information

This site is partially compliant with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines version 2.2 AA standard. This means it meets most of the standard, but not all of it, because of the failures listed below.

Known failures against WCAG 2.2 AA

These are all the failures we know of. Each one names the part of the standard it fails, which is called a success criterion.

  • You cannot use the map's areas with a keyboard. This fails success criterion 2.1.1 (keyboard). The search box, Query, the Directory and Data export give you the same information as text. We are looking at whether we can add a keyboard mode for a shortlist of areas without making the rest of the page harder to use.
  • A custom area can only be built by selecting LSOAs on the map. This also fails 2.1.1 (keyboard).
  • The map's background images have no text version, which fails 1.1.1 (non-text content). They help you see where things are, but they hold no data, and all the data is available as text.
  • Some small bar and distribution charts have no text version of their own, which fails 1.1.1 (non-text content). The figures they show are written out nearby.
  • A PDF saved from the printable reports may not keep the headings and tables that 1.3.1 (info and relationships) asks for.

How this was tested

Before any change to the site goes live, it is checked with axe-core, a tool that looks for accessibility problems automatically. It checks against WCAG 2.2 at levels A and AA, and against axe-core's own good practice rules. A change that fails is not published. The checks cover the map on a desktop and on a phone in the ways people use it most. For example, they check it when it first opens, with an indicator and its card open, with a ward selected, with two indicators shown together and with the phone menu open. They also cover every page around the map.

Automated checks find only about a third of accessibility problems. So we also worked through the map using only a keyboard, on a desktop and at phone width, in the Chromium browser. We moved through every control with Tab, chose an indicator, selected an area from search and from Query, and opened and closed the side panel and its tabs. That is how we found the keyboard failures listed above. The site has not yet been tested with a screen reader or checked by an outside expert in a formal audit. So this statement is based on the checks described here.

What we are doing to improve accessibility

  • Fixing the keyboard failures listed above, then testing the site with a screen reader.
  • Looking at a way into the map itself with a keyboard, most likely for a shortlist of areas rather than every area on the map.
  • Making the custom area builder work without a mouse, by letting you add areas from a list as well as from the map.

This statement was prepared on 17 August 2026 and last reviewed on 19 October 2026, when the site was tested against WCAG 2.2 AA with axe-core and with a keyboard alone in Chromium.
