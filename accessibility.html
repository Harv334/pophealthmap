Accessibility statement

This statement applies to pophealth.uk, an interactive map of population health across England, by region, local authority (LA), ward and neighbourhood. It covers the map itself and every page around it: the methodology, about and privacy, and this statement.

How accessible this site is

You should be able to use most of the site with a keyboard and with a screen reader, and the parts you cannot are listed below in plain terms, with what to use instead.

These things work:

  • Every page can be zoomed to 200% without losing content or function.
  • There is a colour-blind safe palette for the map's colours, which you can switch on with Colour-blind palette under More at the top of the page.
  • Colour is never the only way a figure is given, because everything shaded on the map is also written as a number in the panel and in the downloads.
  • Every control can be reached with the keyboard, and the one with focus is visibly outlined.
  • The toolbar and the sidebar tabs follow standard keyboard navigation patterns: Tab moves to each group as a single stop, and the arrow keys move between the items within it.
  • Selecting a ward, focusing on an LA or an integrated care board (ICB), and zooming to an area are each announced to a screen reader.
  • There is a skip link to the main content on every page.
  • Every indicator has a written card, opened with About this indicator, which explains what is counted, the level it is published at, its period, its source and its method.
  • When you point at or tap an area, the tooltip gives plain-text context beside the figure, such as where it ranks ("higher than 82% of London's LSOAs") and whether a Local Health figure differs significantly from England ("Worse than England, statistically"), so the meaning never relies on colour alone.
  • The two-indicator map uses colour to combine two figures, but both figures are written out when you point at or tap an area, and the key explains each colour in words.

What is not accessible

The map's areas cannot be reached with a keyboard. The shapes on the map, such as wards, MSOAs and LSOAs, respond only to a mouse or touch. Because hundreds of boundaries appear on screen at once, making each one a keyboard stop would leave the rest of the page hundreds of key presses away.

Instead, all of the map's data is available through these keyboard-accessible tools:

  • The search box in the header finds an LA, an ICB, a ward, an LSOA, a GP practice, a pharmacy, a hospital or a charity by name or postcode. Choosing a ward or an LSOA does exactly what selecting it on the map does, and choosing an LA or an ICB focuses the map on it.
  • Query lists the wards matching any set of conditions, as a list you can move through and select from.
  • The Directory holds every service and organisation on the map in one searchable, filterable list.
  • Data export writes the figures for every area to a spreadsheet, so nothing on the map is only on the map.

These are also known to fall short:

Building a custom area needs a mouse or a touch screen
A custom area is built by selecting LSOAs on the map, so it cannot be built with a keyboard. Once it is built you can share it as a link, which opens for anyone, and Data export gives the same figures for published areas without needing the map.
The site has one light colour scheme
The map and the pages around it, including this statement, use one light colour scheme and do not follow a dark setting on your device.
The map's own background is not described
Street names, rivers and parks come from OpenStreetMap and are drawn as images with no text alternative, although nothing this site publishes depends on reading them.
Shading is described in words, not read out area by area
When the map is coloured by an indicator, a screen reader is told what the colours mean and what the range is, but it will not read out every area. Use Query, the lists of the 10 highest and lowest areas, or Data export to get them as an ordered list.
Some charts are images of their data
A few small bar and distribution charts are drawn rather than written out, but every figure in them appears as a number nearby or in the ward's full profile.
The printable ward and LSOA reports may not be fully accessible
The reports open as a separate page for printing or saving as a PDF. Their charts may not have full text alternatives, and a PDF saved from your browser's print dialog may not carry the structure a screen reader needs, so the same figures are also in the area's profile on the map and in Data export.
Answers in the Ask panel are written by AI
The Ask about this data panel answers questions in plain text, and those answers are written by an AI model. They can be wrong or leave things out, so check any figure against the area's profile or the downloads before relying on it. We have not checked how the panel works with a screen reader.

Reporting accessibility problems

If you find a problem that is not listed here, or you need something on this site in a different format, email contact@pophealth.uk and say which page it was and what you were trying to do. We aim to reply within five working days.

If you ask for information in an accessible format and you are not happy with our response, say so in your reply and we will look at it again.

Technical information

This site is partially compliant with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines version 2.2 AA standard, because of the failures listed below.

Known failures against WCAG 2.2 AA

These are all the failures we know of, each with the success criterion it fails.

  • The map's areas cannot be operated with a keyboard, which fails success criterion 2.1.1 (keyboard). The search box, Query, the Directory and Data export give a text route to the same information, and we are looking at whether a keyboard mode over a shortlist of areas can be added without making the rest of the page harder to use.
  • A custom area can only be built by selecting LSOAs on the map, which also fails 2.1.1 (keyboard).
  • The map's background images carry no text alternative, which fails 1.1.1 (non-text content). They are there for context rather than data, and the data is all available as text.
  • Some small bar and distribution charts have no text alternative of their own, which fails 1.1.1 (non-text content), although the figures they show are written out nearby.
  • The printable reports may not meet 1.1.1 (non-text content) for their charts, and a PDF saved from them may not keep the headings and tables that 1.3.1 (info and relationships) asks for.

How this was tested

Before any change to the site is published, it is checked with axe-core, an automated accessibility checker, against WCAG 2.2 at levels A and AA and against axe-core's own best-practice rules, and a change that fails is not published. The checks cover the map on a desktop and on a phone in the states people use most, such as on opening, with an indicator and its card open, with a ward selected, with two indicators drawn together and with the phone menu open, as well as every page around the map.

Automated checks find only about a third of accessibility problems, so we also checked by hand that every control on the map can be reached with the keyboard in a sensible order, that the one with focus is always outlined, and that you can choose an indicator and open and close a theme with the keyboard alone. The site has not been through a formal external audit, so this statement is based on the checks described here.

What we are doing to improve accessibility

  • Looking at a keyboard route into the map itself, most likely over a shortlist of areas rather than every area on the map.
  • Making the custom area builder usable without a mouse, by letting you add areas from a list as well as from the map.
